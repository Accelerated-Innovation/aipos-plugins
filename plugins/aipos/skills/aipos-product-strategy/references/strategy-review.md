# Choosing, assessing, and revising strategy

## Make choices explicit

Ask what would make this product worth choosing and what must be given up to
focus. Separate initial users from buyers, sponsors, administrators, and other
stakeholders. Use descriptive roles; do not invent persona biographies.

When several segments are supplied, compare them using what is actually known:
need and consequence, access to participants, switching friction, ability to
reach a buyer or sponsor, organizational fit, and viability constraints. Use
unknown cells rather than fabricated scores or a made-up total market. Offer a
reasoned proposed focus when asked and state what would reverse it. If choosing
would be premature, make segment comparison the next research question and
leave the initial segment undecided. “All of them” needs an explicit shared need
and a credible adoption route, not simply a longer persona list.

Treat a solution-shaped request as useful context. Recover the job, present
behavior, difficulty, and desired change. Keep the proposed technology in the
concept, not the need. If a technology or vendor is already mandated, retain that
constraint and assess the remaining choices; do not reopen an adopted constraint
merely because the skill prefers open exploration.

## Commercial and internal viability

| Commercial product | Internal product |
|---|---|
| Who uses, chooses, and pays? | Who uses, sponsors, funds, and operates it? |
| What makes switching worth the cost? | What makes changing current work worthwhile? |
| How could pricing and revenue support material costs? | How would benefits be realized, and by whom? |
| How could customers be reached and retained? | What adoption, training, integration, and change effort is needed? |
| What sales, support, partner, and operating constraints matter? | What support, ownership, compliance, and operating constraints matter? |

These are hypotheses to investigate, not a demand for a completed business case.
Do not equate interest with purchase, signup with sustained use, mandatory usage
with value, or recovered time with cash savings. Preserve stated strategic fit;
do not invent a company objective, revenue model, or financial return.

## Pick the uncertainty that most changes the decision

Consider Cagan's four risk categories without forcing four experiments:

- **Value:** Will the intended users choose it or buyers pay? What alternative
  would they actually leave, including their current workaround?
- **Usability:** Could users achieve the intended result in the proposed experience?
- **Feasibility:** Can engineering deliver it within relevant technical, data,
  access, and resource constraints?
- **Business viability:** Would it work for the organization's economics, route
  to adoption, obligations, and operations?

Ask the relevant people: users/buyers for value, users and design for usability,
engineering for feasibility, and affected business stakeholders for viability.
Do not claim those consultations occurred merely because a brief names them.
For AI concepts, include applicable quality, trust, data-access, evaluation, and
cost uncertainties; delegate experimental evaluation design to rapid-validation.

Prioritize by consequence if wrong and uncertainty, considering the cost of
learning. Explain the choice without inventing numerical risk scores. Name the
observation that would alter the choice and delegate the appropriate interview,
sizing, demand, prototype, or feasibility artifact. A field being blank is not
automatically the most important risk.

## Review results against the strategy

1. Read the current brief and the new sources. Identify the claim or decision each
   finding bears on, its segment, period, measure, and comparison.
2. Separate what happened from why it happened. A pilot can show a result without
   proving its cause, generalizing to another segment, or demonstrating retention.
3. Compare against an adopted criterion if one exists. Without one, report the
   finding and the missing decision; do not retrospectively invent a pass bar.
4. Explain what is supported, contradicted, or still uncertain. Address negative
   results, sampling limits, source dependence, and measurement changes.
5. Recommend the smallest justified strategy change—or retain the hypothesis and
   test a specific uncertainty. If evidence undermines the central value or
   viability premise, include pausing or ending the bet as real options.
6. Record a proposed change with its reason and source. Once adopted, preserve the
   former choice in revision history, update the canonical brief, and name the
   downstream experiments/initiatives/commitments whose owners must review it.

A request to revise the document authorizes preparing the revision; it does not
by itself supply stakeholder agreement on every newly proposed strategic choice.
Preserve supplied adopted decisions and distinguish changes still awaiting one.
Do not silently rewrite committed behavior or revoke/reissue approvals.

One measurable outcome is sufficient when longer-term sequencing is speculative.
Identify how and when the team will revisit it, using supplied cadence or a clearly
proposed review trigger. This skill interprets product results to evolve strategy;
it does not install analytics or infer customer outcomes from delivery throughput.
